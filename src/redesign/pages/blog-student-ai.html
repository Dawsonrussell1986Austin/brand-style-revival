Who decided your students would get AI?

October 6, 2026 · Bob Hutchins

A response from the ACES Center for AI to The Wall Street Journal's investigation of Google's AI in schools.

The Wall Street Journal opened its investigation of Google's AI in schools with a Pennsylvania 8th grader. For a year, the Journal reports, he pasted his algebra problems into the search bar on his school laptop. Then he failed the final.

Reporters Shalini Ramachandran, Georgia Wells and Molly Reinmann spoke with more than 60 people, among them teachers, school officials, parents, students and clinicians. They also reviewed more than a dozen Google research papers and report that the company's own researchers have written about higher risks for children.

We work on AI in Connecticut schools every day because we believe every student should graduate understanding how these systems work. The harm the Journal describes is real.

The ACES Center for AI exists to help schools move beyond just AI adoption. Since August, access to AI has reached many students ahead of the teaching that should come with it.

How access arrived

In August, Google turned on a Gemini tab in Google Classroom by default for many K-12 student accounts. Robbie Torney of Common Sense Media wrote that the change applied where schools had already given students some Gemini access, and that several administrators told The New York Times they learned of it afterward.

Google Search now answers with AI too. Common Sense says no one can turn that off, administrators included. The Journal reports that Los Angeles and Fairfax County, Virginia, each built a workaround of their own.

Once Gemini is on, Torney adds, administrators can't keep students from bringing it personal or mental health topics.

We'd call this adoption by update. A district takes on a classroom technology because a vendor changed a setting, and the decision reaches the superintendent as news.

Our own site says schools "need more than access to new technologies." In every central office this month, someone should ask who decided our students would have this, and when.

The evidence

Google has evidence on its side.

On June 9, 2026, Google DeepMind and Fab AI published results from a preregistered randomized trial in Sierra Leone. The trial ran 8 weeks and covered 1,763 junior secondary students in 12 schools. Students who used Gemini's Guided Learning mode in math class gained 0.258 standard deviations against the control group.

Teachers led those lessons, though. The trial's design limited it to students 13 and older. Google's spokeswoman told the Journal that, according to studies, students can learn more when AI is paired with instruction led by a teacher.

The OECD released its PISA 2025 results on September 8, after testing more than 760,000 15-year-olds in 91 countries and economies.

Students who used AI for specific schoolwork tasks scored lower in science than students who didn't. Those who used AI often tended to do slightly better when their schools also gave them chances to build AI literacy.

Those chances go more often to advantaged students, the OECD found. In the United States, 56.3% of students said they learn to assess AI-generated information in their lessons, against an OECD average of 62.6% (the OECD flags U.S. survey results for caution because of a coverage issue).

The OECD calls the relationship complex. A survey like this shows association, so it can't prove what caused what.

In the trial and in the survey, the results that favor AI came with teaching.

Where we begin with AI

Our curriculum's website says preparing students "requires more than learning how to use AI tools." They have to understand how these systems work.

That work begins in kindergarten, where students don't use AI tools on their own. In a kindergarten activity we shared for National AI Literacy Day, the teacher announces that she's a robot.

A child tells her to pick up the pencil, and she picks up the wrong thing on purpose. The class works out what went wrong and tries a better direction. By the end, kindergartners have learned that a machine does exactly what it's told.

We mark that lesson A0 on the Center's AI Integration Continuum, which runs from A0 to A5. Its guiding principle reads, "AI systems may support thinking. They may not replace it." A0 (zero hands on use of AI) can be required any time a task is meant to show independent reasoning.

The continuum's own descriptive text says it is designed to prevent passive cognitive offloading.

Connecticut already makes teenagers learn before they drive. A 16-year-old who goes through a driving school holds a learner's permit for at least 120 days and sits through 30 hours of classroom instruction.

There are 40 hours of practice driving before the road test, and a parent has to take a 2-hour class too. We think AI in schools should work the same way.

In September, New York City put a 1-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI through 8th grade. A district could do the same and still teach AI literacy in every one of those grades, because the first 2 levels of our continuum involve no direct AI use by students.

Starting in 2027-28, the College Board will teach AI directly in AP Computer Science Principles and have AP Seminar students write their scored essays in a secure setting with no generative AI.

Our part in this

The Center builds AI tools, so we're part of this story. Our Curriculum Studio helps educators design units and lessons. Our newsletter, The Human Loop, has told readers to paste its weekly prompt into Gemini or another chatbot.

We stand by that work, and we see a difference between those users and a student. A teacher already knows how to plan a lesson before she asks AI for help, while a student handed the answer to an algebra problem skips the work that teaches him algebra.

For 2 years, most of the talk in our field was about acquisition, about which tool to buy and from which vendor. The Journal's reporting is a fair account of what that talk left out.

What we're asking for

Superintendents: find out which AI features are live in student accounts today, by grade band, and which ones your admin console can't turn off. Then put a name next to each decision. If you'd like help, ACES offers districts a complimentary Digital Learning & AI Impact Audit. We study how your district is using technology and AI as well as determining how it connects to instruction, connecting to what we know to be true about teaching and learning as educators.

Teachers: decide what the assignment is for before you decide about AI. If it's meant to show what a student can do alone, it's A0. Our guide, Designing Assessments in an AI-Influenced World, starts with the thinking that should "remain visible and human."

Vendors: ship student-facing AI features off by default, and tell districts before a default changes. Administrators also need a switch for the AI answers in Search.

Connecticut: since July 1, state law has listed computer science, including artificial intelligence, in the program of instruction every public school must offer. Beginning January 1, 2027, the same law (Public Act 26-15) requires operators of AI companions to have measures in place that keep those products from manipulating users under 18 into inappropriate emotional dependence. The State Department of Education has been drafting AI guidance, and we'd ask that it put literacy ahead of access in the early grades.

Getting Started

MIT's Justin Reich told MassLive that every district is now running an experiment, then asked how carefully each one checks its results, a test the Center has to pass too.

Our work rests on keeping human thinking at the center of every decision. That includes the default settings on a student's account. So this week, find out what's switched on in your district and who chose it.

A child's first lesson about AI should come from a human teacher. The login can wait.
