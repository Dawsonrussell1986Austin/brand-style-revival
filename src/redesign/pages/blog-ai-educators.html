The Future of AI in Education Must Be Shaped by Educators, Not Technology Companies

An ACES Perspective on Google's New AI Policy, District Autonomy, and Preparing Students for an AI-Driven Future

November 11, 2026 · By ACES Professional Development and School Improvement (PDSI) and the ACES Center for Artificial Intelligence

Preparing students for an AI-driven future is our responsibility. Deciding how and when they engage with AI must remain our choice. On November 3, 2026, Google announced significant changes to how K–12 schools control student access to its artificial intelligence tools and search engine. While the company presented these changes as providing schools with greater control, the announcement raised an important question for educators, school leaders, and families:

Who should determine when students are ready to engage with artificial intelligence, educational communities or technology companies?

Under Google's newly announced approach, schools will be able to independently enable products such as Gemini, Gemini Notebook, and Google Search for student accounts. However, while districts can choose to enable Search without enabling the standalone Gemini applications, Google does not currently offer an option to retain traditional Search without its integrated AI capabilities, including AI Overviews. Districts that choose not to enable AI-enhanced Search will therefore lose access to Google Search through school-managed student accounts when the new controls begin rolling out in early 2027.

Google has explained that its intention is to provide more educationally appropriate AI experiences, including Guided Learning features designed to help students work through problems rather than simply generate answers. Google maintains that its revised controls are intended to give school administrators greater choice over which AI-enabled services students can access, with those services disabled by default until administrators authorize them.

We recognize the potential educational value of these innovations. However, we believe that the integration of AI into existing educational tools should not come at the expense of district autonomy.

As ACES Deputy Executive Director Tim Howes emphasizes, embracing innovation and preserving educational autonomy are not competing priorities.

“At ACES, we believe AI has tremendous potential to improve education, but technology should never drive educational decisions. Those decisions belong to educators and school communities who know their students best. Our goal is to help districts embrace the opportunities AI offers while making sure students and learning always come first.”

— Tim Howes, Deputy Executive Director, ACES

AI Readiness Is Essential. So Is Educational Autonomy.

We are not opposed to artificial intelligence in education. Quite the opposite. Through the ACES Center for Artificial Intelligence, we believe AI will fundamentally influence how students learn, how educators teach, and how individuals participate in the workforce and society. We also believe that preparing students for this future is an educational responsibility we cannot ignore.

Connecticut Governor Ned Lamont has demonstrated a clear commitment to positioning Connecticut as a leader in AI innovation and workforce readiness. In June 2026, Governor Lamont signed Public Act 26-15, legislation that advances both AI innovation and protections against potential harms. The legislation establishes the Connecticut AI Academy, expands opportunities for workforce preparation, and promotes responsible AI development.

The Governor's broader workforce initiatives, including the Connecticut Tech Talent Accelerator and Career Pathways Commission, recognize that emerging technologies are transforming the skills students will need for their futures.

This direction is one we strongly support. However, there is an important distinction between preparing students to thrive in an AI-driven world and requiring students to use AI tools before their schools have determined they are educationally appropriate.

AI readiness cannot be measured simply by whether students have access to a chatbot or an AI-enhanced search engine.

It must include the ability to think critically, evaluate information, recognize bias, protect personal information, understand the capabilities and limitations of AI, and make responsible decisions about when technology supports learning and when independent thinking is more valuable. Districts must have the flexibility to determine the developmental progression of these skills, the tools students use, and the safeguards that accompany implementation.

State leadership can establish a shared vision for AI readiness while respecting the role of local educational communities in determining how that vision becomes reality. We can be committed to preparing every Connecticut student for an AI-driven workforce while also recognizing that not every student, grade level, or district needs the same AI tools at the same time.

The Research Calls for Intentionality, Not Urgency

The urgency to prepare students for an AI-driven future is real. So are the emerging concerns about the ways students are currently engaging with artificial intelligence. An August 2026 study from Common Sense Media found that 70% of surveyed teenagers ages 13-17 reported using AI for schoolwork. Among those students, 63% reported using AI to obtain answers to their assignments. Yet only 27% reported having discussed with a teacher what AI is and how it works.

The findings illustrate a challenge facing schools: Students are adopting AI tools faster than many educational systems can establish the instruction, expectations, and safeguards necessary for responsible use. The study also revealed important benefits. Many teens reported using AI for brainstorming, checking their work, and understanding difficult concepts. Similarly, research published by RAND in March 2026 documented increasing student use of AI for homework alongside uncertainty about how students should appropriately use these tools. These findings do not justify wholesale rejection of AI, nor do they support unrestricted access. Instead, they underscore the importance of intentional implementation.

Students need opportunities to develop foundational knowledge and skills before relying on technologies that may perform parts of the thinking process for them. They need to learn how to ask questions, locate and evaluate sources, develop arguments, solve problems, and engage in productive struggle. AI can eventually enhance many of these learning experiences. But it should not replace the cognitive work necessary to develop these abilities.

Access to AI is not the same as AI literacy. And introducing AI earlier is not necessarily the same as preparing students better.

A National Conversation About Who Controls Educational Technology

Google's announcement has prompted concern from educators, policymakers, and children's advocacy organizations. Connecticut Senator Richard Blumenthal criticized the company's policy, questioning whether Google's approach prioritizes the adoption of AI products over student learning. Former Federal Trade Commission Chair Lina Khan similarly raised concerns about Google's market influence and its ability to shape schools' technology decisions. Yvette Renteria, Chief Program Officer at Common Sense Media, also raised concerns about the absence of a traditional Google Search option without integrated AI, particularly for districts seeking greater control over students' exposure to AI. These responses reflect a broader concern that extends well beyond one company or one product.

As educational technology becomes increasingly interconnected, school districts must retain meaningful control over their instructional environments. We recognize and value the significant contributions technology companies have made to teaching and learning. Google and other providers have developed tools that expand access to information, support collaboration, and create new educational opportunities. Yet educational partnerships work best when technology providers respond to the needs and decisions of schools, not when product design determines the choices available to them.

Schools should not have to choose between preserving access to an essential learning resource and maintaining their educational judgment about AI integration.

Districts Have Options: Exploring Alternative Search Tools

Districts that determine AI-enhanced Google Search is not appropriate for some or all students should know that alternatives exist. Importantly, no search engine is inherently risk-free or guaranteed to be appropriate for every student. Before selecting an alternative, districts should evaluate filtering capabilities, privacy protections, advertising, accessibility, integration with existing systems, and whether AI-generated responses can be disabled.

The following options provide potential starting points for district technology teams and educational leaders.

These are options for evaluation, not blanket endorsements or assurances that any provider fully satisfies student privacy and safety requirements.

For districts considering alternatives, we recommend examining five questions:

  1. Student safety: Can explicit and inappropriate content be reliably filtered, and can those settings be enforced on school-managed devices?
  2. Student privacy: What information is collected, stored, shared, or used for advertising or AI model improvement? Does the service satisfy applicable student privacy requirements?
  3. AI controls: Can students search and evaluate original sources without automatically receiving AI-generated summaries? Can AI features be restricted by age or grade level?
  4. Technical feasibility: Can the search engine be implemented across existing Chromebooks, browsers, networks, and student accounts without creating new security or accessibility concerns?
  5. Instructional value: Does the tool support the research, inquiry, source evaluation, and information-literacy skills students need to develop?

Districts should also recognize that Safe Search settings are only one layer of protection. Comprehensive safeguards may include network and device filtering, approved resource lists, direct instruction, ongoing monitoring, and family communication. The solution should not simply be to replace one technology provider with another. It should be to select tools that align with educational goals and provide schools with meaningful control.

Our Commitment: Pedagogy First, Innovation With Purpose

At ACES Professional Development and School Improvement and the ACES Center for Artificial Intelligence, our work is grounded in a simple belief:

Innovation has educational value when it strengthens teaching, learning, and human connection.

Through our commitment to learning, innovation, and transformation, we approach AI integration as an educational design challenge, not simply a technology adoption process. Our work supports educators, school leaders, students, and families in building the knowledge and capacity necessary to make informed decisions about AI. This includes three connected commitments:

  • Building AI-ready educators and school communities. We support leaders in examining readiness, developing responsible policies, and engaging stakeholders. We help educators understand how AI can support instruction while preserving critical thinking, student agency, and authentic learning.
  • Developing AI literacy through intentional learning progressions. Our ACES K–12 AI Literacy Curriculum emphasizes foundational AI literacy through offline learning experiences in grades K–5, allowing younger students to understand how AI works, examine ethical questions, and develop critical thinking without requiring direct interaction with generative AI tools. In grades 6–12, the curriculum provides opportunities for increasingly sophisticated and intentional engagement with AI, when districts determine it is appropriate.
  • Advancing responsible innovation, research, and ethical practice. We support districts in evaluating emerging technologies, examining potential risks, and making evidence-informed decisions about implementation.

We also recognize the enormous potential of AI to support the work of educators. When used thoughtfully, AI can serve as a thought partner in lesson design, differentiation, assessment development, and instructional planning. It can help automate routine tasks and reduce administrative burdens, creating greater opportunities for educators to devote their time and expertise to students, families, and school communities. We believe this is where AI can offer some of its greatest educational value: not by replacing the work of educators, but by strengthening their capacity to do the work that matters most.

This commitment reflects ACES' broader belief that educational innovation must always begin with the needs of learners and the professional expertise of educators. As Dr. William Rice, Executive Director of ACES, explains:

“At ACES, we believe decisions about when and how students engage with artificial intelligence must begin with the learner, not the technology. AI holds tremendous promise for education, but that promise can only be realized when its use is intentional, developmentally appropriate, and grounded in human relationships, professional judgment, and authentic learning. Technology companies should support the educational decisions of schools and communities, not make those decisions for them.”

— Dr. William Rice, Executive Director, ACES

Moving Forward: AI-Ready Does Not Mean AI-Rushed

We are living through a period of profound technological change, and public education has a responsibility to prepare students for the world they will inherit. Connecticut's leadership in AI innovation and workforce development provides an important opportunity for schools to rethink how we prepare students for college, careers, and civic life. But preparing students for the future cannot come at the expense of the foundational skills they need today.

The question is not whether AI belongs in education. The more important questions are:

  • What educational purpose does AI serve?
  • When is its use developmentally appropriate?
  • How do we ensure it strengthens rather than replaces student thinking?
  • Who participates in decisions about student access?
  • What safeguards must be in place before implementation?

These questions should be answered through collaboration among educators, district leaders, families, students, communities, and state education partners, guided by research and educational expertise. Technology companies have an important role in this work, but they should not determine the pace or direction of educational change. If a technology provider cannot offer the flexibility necessary to support a district's educational decisions, districts should be empowered to evaluate other options.

At ACES, we remain committed to helping Connecticut districts navigate this evolving landscape with clarity, confidence, and purpose. We will continue advocating for innovation that is grounded in pedagogy, guided by evidence, centered on students, and shaped by the communities our schools serve. Because the future of learning should not be determined by the technology available to us. It should be determined by the future we want for our students.
